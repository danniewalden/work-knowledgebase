# Is it safe to jump?

*(Christian Johansen, ChronosHub — LinkedIn post, ~2 days before retrieval; surfaced via Martin Dilger's repost. Tags: #eventmodeling #specdriven #agentic #chronoshub. Verbatim.)*

Is it safe to jump?

We arrived in Munich for the Event Modeling Conference 2026 — right in the middle of a heatwave. The newspapers had one word on their front pages: Hitzewelle. Extreme heat. The city was sweating.

In the English Garden, right in the middle of Munich, there's a side branch of the Isar river called the Eisbach. During a heatwave, jumping in is enormously tempting. It's officially prohibited. We'd seen references to drowning deaths, eight people mentioned somewhere, though we had no idea over how many years or how many thousands had taken the plunge. On day two, we went to see for ourselves. Hundreds of people were already in the water. The risk suddenly felt manageable. So we jumped.

The question on everyone's lips at the conference was a different kind of jump: why are only a handful of us doing this — and how do we bring the rest of the software world along?

The community is doing something remarkable. But it struggles to tell a coherent story about itself. It is fragmented — Event Sourcing, CQRS, DDD, Event Modeling, Domain Storytelling — each camp with its own vocabulary, its own canonical examples, its own conventions. Explaining event modeling to an enterprise today can feel like teaching someone to become a mechanic rather than just handing them the keys to a car. And if event modeling is to grow, the community needs to speak to leadership — not just to engineers.

At ChronosHub, we find ourselves in a rare position: we already have leadership buy-in for an engineering strategy built on event modeling and event sourcing. The conference didn't change our direction, but it sharpened our thinking. We came away with new nuances, richer conversations with practitioners doing serious work, and — perhaps most valuably — a reinforced conviction that our architecture and strategy for the agentic future are pointing in the right direction.

One open question stayed with me: how does event modeling intersect with experience engineering — not just designing systems, but crafting interaction experiences that work seamlessly for both humans and AI agents? We're already building the systems. The harder, more interesting question is how to shape the experience layer on top of them. No clean answers yet, but the right people are asking the right questions.

The broader lesson from Munich still applies: sometimes you need to see hundreds of people already in the water before you feel safe to jump.
We jumped. It was worth it.
