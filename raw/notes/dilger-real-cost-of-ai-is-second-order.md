I spoke with one CTO and he told me his engineering costs had climbed almost 30% while his team size stayed flat. He assumed it was the AI tooling bill. It wasn't. The AI tooling did add to the cost, but in a much smaller percentage. The rest was hiding somewhere nobody thinks to look.

So, where was the money actually going? When he traced it, the increase broke into pieces that never appear as a line item. Incident response hours, up sharply. More incidents, and each taking longer to diagnose because the code involved was less understood. Onboarding cost, up. New hires taking longer than expected to become productive, which is real salary spent on people not yet contributing. Senior engineer time reallocated from building to firefighting, which doesn't change payroll but silently converts team's most expensive people from asset to overhead. And the quiet one: features that took three attempts instead of one, because the first two collided with things nobody knew were there.

None of that shows up as "AI cost." It shows up as engineering being generally more expensive, in ways that feel like bad luck rather than a pattern.

The reframe that landed for him: he'd been evaluating AI adoption on its direct cost: licenses, tokens, tooling, and measuring its benefit in velocity. But the actual economics were being decided in second-order costs he wasn't tracking at all. On the tooling line, AI looked cheap and productive. On the full ledger, it was expensive and roughly break-even.

Once he could see the whole picture, the decision got easy. Not "stop using AI" (nobody sane suggests that). But "fix the things converting our speed into cost," which turned out to be a much smaller, more tractable problem than his rising budget suggested.

The lesson for anyone approving next year's engineering budget: if your costs are climbing and your headcount isn't, the answer isn't in your tooling invoices. It's in the second-order costs nobody assigned an owner to.

What's your real cost of AI? Not the bill, the ledger?
