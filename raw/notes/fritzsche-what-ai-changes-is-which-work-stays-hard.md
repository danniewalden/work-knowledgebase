Something I've noticed very often - both in team discussions over the years and here on LinkedIn - is that discussions quickly shift to implementation details. But these details will become less and less critical.

What I generally find lacking is an understanding of the problem and the domain. If the actual implementation becomes cheaper and faster, then people who are able to figure out what actually needs to be built will become more important. By that I mean that the question of which rules apply and what consequences a technical decision has will become increasingly important. In the next years, this will have to be an essential trait of a developer.

And as I keep reading and hearing, there's a big misunderstanding here: "A solid foundation in a main programming language and frameworks." That doesn't convince me at all, because a framework isn't a foundation. So if you're proficient in ASP.NET Core, Spring, React, and so on, then you're proficient in tools. These are interchangeable implementation options. And if you focus on them, then you yourself become interchangeable.

The fundamentals are system design, reliability, data, consistency, system structure and boundaries, and so on. With this, the focus shifts more toward system design, architecture, and true engineering work.

In other words: These days, it's no longer (and I guess it never was) relevant to draw a pretty picture made up of circles, call it "Clean Architecture" or "Onions," and claim that's what architecture is. Building systems is about understanding a problem and finding the best solution.

Nothing has changed in that regard.

What AI changes is which part of that work remains difficult.

The only difference is that, thanks to AI agents, it's becoming increasingly clear that developers often created and solved problems that had absolutely nothing to do with the domain or the business problem.
