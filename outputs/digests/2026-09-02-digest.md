### 1. Rico Fritzsche — "Why the Entity Model Is an Illusion"
*ricofritzsche.me, 2026-08-26 · `raw/articles/fritzsche-why-the-entity-model-is-an-illusion.md` · **filed, not yet ingested***

**TLDR.** Fritzsche's argument is that the database row for a "Vehicle" is not the vehicle — it is only the residue of the last write, which is why nearly every system grows audit tables, history tables, status columns and change logs as workarounds. His alternative starts from what happens: registering a vehicle is not `CreateVehicle` (a database operation wearing domain clothes) but a sequence — `VehicleAcquired → VehicleReceived → VehicleInfleeted → VehicleReleasedForRental` — and the "Vehicle" is then whatever **projection** someone asks for, built when they ask. The connective tissue is a reference (an ID, or a natural key like a VIN), not a pre-declared entity. And the definition that matters for your focus: *"A domain capability is something like 'AcquireVehicle' or 'ReceiveVehicle.' They are independent of one another. They are autonomous, self-contained, and coherent. They share only the Application State… However, they know nothing about each other."* Mutating state in place, he says, costs two things: it destroys the information about what happened, and it turns every concurrent request into a conflict you have to defend against.

**Why it's worth reading.** It is the **capability-decomposition claim stated from the data-modelling side**, and it falsifies yesterday's open thread that the flagged-important business-capabilities line has "zero outstanding raw material" — this file has been sitting in `raw/` since 08-27. The word "illusion" appears nowhere in the wiki outside Böckeler's context-engineering page, and no concept page carries the entity-vs-projection framing at all, so this is a genuine gap rather than a restatement of the twelve Fritzsche pages already compiled. It also supplies the *why* under [[autonomous-domain-capabilities]] that the CCC pages assume.

### 2. Nick Tune — "Enforced application architecture for agents and humans"
*nick-tune.me, 2026-08-13 · `raw/articles/nick-tune-enforced-application-architecture-agents-humans.md` · **filed, not yet ingested — captured 08-17, 16 days waiting***

**TLDR.** Tune's opening line is the whole thesis: AI-generated code does not follow architectural guidelines *"written in skill files, ADRs, and various other places in the repo,"* so he stopped writing them down and started making the build fail. He describes a working DSL (Rivière) with three tiers of enforcement: **package classification** (a `packages/{subdomain}/domain-model` package may have exactly one `/domain` root, cannot import other subdomains, and may only import versioned *published-language* contracts); **layer rules** (feature folders are vertical slices under `/features/{feature}` with `/commands`, `/queries`, `/data-access`, `/adapters` and no sharing between features); and **role rules** as a last resort — an adapter may import from `/domain` but only things annotated `domain-port`, data-access only `aggregate` and `value-object`, never `domain-service`, because *"AI does dumb stuff like that all the time even with a million lines of markdown screaming at it not to do that."* Roles can be constrained down to shape (private constructor, `parse`-prefixed static, branded member), which he argues makes the wrong thing structurally unimplementable rather than merely discouraged. He is candid about the cost: package/domain/layer rules he'd never skip; annotating every file with a role is *"still playing around with that. Come back in 6 months."* Note the ADR detail — **ADR-002 and the executable Rivière config describe the same architecture and are kept aligned by hand.**

**TLDR (2).** *Interested-party note per CLAUDE.md: Rivière is Tune's own tool and the post closes with "if you want help adding this to your codebase, get in touch" — the effectiveness claims are vendor self-report.*

**Why it's worth reading.** This is the **third position in the [[model-as-code-vs-model-as-language]] argument**, and neither Miller nor Dilger occupies it: the model is neither prose nor code but a *build constraint*, and markdown's failure is taken as the starting premise rather than the thing to be debated. It's also the concrete mechanism behind [[adr]]'s MADR "Confirmation" section (an acceptance criterion in all but name — Tune actually wires it up), it grounds the [[slice]] page's code-unit sense in an enforced layout, and nothing in `wiki/` mentions Rivière or enforced architecture outside the log.

### 4. Oskar Dudycz — "Fixing bugs in Event Sourcing is hard, for real?"
*Architecture Weekly / event-driven.io, 2026-07-27 · `raw/articles/dudycz-fixing-bugs-in-event-sourcing.md` · **filed, not yet ingested — captured 08-10, 23 days waiting, second-oldest in the queue***

**TLDR.** A worked incident rather than an argument. A tourist-tax rate change ships a bug; the state-based fix matches 1,240 rows, of which only ~900 are actually broken, and because `recalculate_total` reads *today's* rate plan it silently reprices ~400 reservations booked before a seasonal increase — so 400 guests get a confirmation higher than what they agreed to. Migration two must then be written against data migration one produced, with booking dates reconstructed from confirmation emails. The event-sourced version of the same bug is boring: the 14 March events still say what they said, `RatePlanApplied` still names version 4 at 180.00, so the wrong `cityTax` is computable without reconstructing anything. Two moves carry the piece. First, **`buildSha` in event metadata** — the commit the service was running when it appended — turns "which rows are wrong?" into a `WHERE metadata->>'buildSha' = 'a4f9c2e'` that returns exactly the 900. Second, **fix forward with a corrective event** rather than editing history, the accountant's move: *"we don't rub out a ledger line; we post a correcting entry next to it."* The best part is the human case — a front-office clerk already corrected one reservation by hand, taking 42.00 off the rooms as goodwill, and a blind bulk recalculation would silently undo her; with the stream you can branch on "a correction already follows the bad event" and route it to a person. His conclusion: model `CorrectReservationPrice` as a real command with permissions and a mandatory reason *before* you need it, because otherwise *"someone will do it with an `UPDATE` at 23:00."*

**Why it's worth reading.** It's the substrate thread's strongest **operational** argument, and the wiki has none — `buildSha`, corrective events and fix-forward appear nowhere in `wiki/`. It also lands squarely on unattended agents: a corrective-event discipline is exactly what makes an overnight agent's mistakes recoverable rather than compounding, which is the concern [[unattended-coding-agents]] was rewritten around on 08-31 and currently answers only with verifier sub-agents.

### 5. Rachel Laycock (CTO, Thoughtworks) — "Citizens Build, Agents Execute, Experts Govern"
*martinfowler.com / Rachel's Ramblings, 2026-08-19 · `raw/articles/laycock-citizens-build-agents-execute-experts-govern.md` · **filed, not yet ingested***

**TLDR.** Laycock starts from the executive question — *"If AI can do this now, why aren't our engineering teams delivering ten times faster?"* — and answers that we conflated writing software with software engineering, and did it to ourselves. The weekend app is real software; what changes the moment a business depends on it is the set of questions nobody asks in a demo: is customer data protected, what happens when a dependency fails, will it survive an audit, how do we know something is wrong before customers do. Her reframing of scarcity is the sharp bit: we spent decades optimising around people who could write code, and *"what feels scarce now is good engineering judgement."* Hence the slogan, which she explicitly says is **not about roles** but about where value moves — experts become "dramatically more leveraged," building the guardrails, platforms and feedback loops in which others and agents can work safely. She also reports from FOSE a team that spends the day designing a specification, lets agents run overnight, and reviews in the morning — noting that the interesting part was not the pipeline but the humans *"deciding what good looked like."* Closing line: *"Organisations don't run on code. They run on trust."* She flags one antipattern by name: citizens building and throwing it to engineers to fix.

**TLDR (2).** *Not-independent note per CLAUDE.md: Laycock is Thoughtworks' CTO writing on martinfowler.com, so this is not external corroboration for Thoughtworks-originated framings (including the capability-model work Batch B compiled).*

**Why it's worth reading.** [[business-capabilities]] currently arrives via data architecture (Sadalage/Chandrasekaran) and via Fritzsche's code-level decomposition; this is the **org-design and governance rung** of the same ladder, from the org whose capability model the page is built on — and Laycock is cited nowhere in the wiki except one line on [[thoughtworks]].
