### 1. Andrew Miracle — "My Loop Engineering Workflow" (andrewmiracle.com, 2026-08-10)
`raw/articles/miracle-my-loop-engineering-workflow.md` — *filed 08-21, not yet ingested*

**TLDR.** Miracle (Head of Product & Research at Tecmie) argues the prompt is ~5% of the outcome and the loops are the rest, then documents his actual harness — public at `github.com/koolamusic/claudefiles`, so the claims are inspectable. The shape is a **triangle of nested loops** running one identical grammar at three time scales: session (hours; opens with a written commission, closes with a structured closeout/handoff), sprint (days; research → plan → execute → retro), program (weeks; PROJECT.md + phased ROADMAP.md → packets). The grammar is *objective → execution → feedback → refined objective*, and it's the refinement step that "is why the loop converges instead of spinning." Notable primitives: **fork-join where "the join is a gate, not a barrier — it doesn't merge output, it merges confidence"**; Amdahl's law applied to agents (generation parallelised, verification didn't, so the serial work *moved to the join* rather than shrinking); three sequenced non-advisory gates (plan-checker with planner and checker never sharing a context, a Nyquist-style sampling test, and a **goal-backward verifier** — "for every requirement in the plan, where in the diff is it satisfied? Any requirement without evidence fails the audit, even if all the tests pass"); a **trust ledger** on every tool call with Fibonacci-scaled deductions and capability levels; bounded audit loops; and "documents over conversations" at every handoff.

**Why it's worth reading.** The goal-backward verifier is exactly the rubric a GWT-per-slice event model would supply — it's the strongest evidence yet for the Event Modeling ↔ loop-engineering seam argued *from the loop side*, and his explicit-context-layers prescription is a near-independent rediscovery of the LLM-wiki pattern this knowledgebase itself runs on.

### 3. Oskar Dudycz — "Vertical slices, their ownership and external dependencies" (Event-Driven.io, 2026-08-10)
`raw/articles/dudycz-vertical-slices-ownership-and-external-dependencies.md` — *filed 08-15, not yet ingested*

**TLDR.** Dudycz takes the five questions that keep recurring about vertical slices ("a table per slice?", "are seven areas seven bounded contexts?", "how can *verify order* read what *register order* wrote if they mustn't know about each other?") and shows they're all one question: **what does a slice do when it needs something from outside?** He names the unstated assumption doing the damage — that a slice ought to be self-contained, so needing anything external means the cut was wrong — and rejects it: "minimise isn't zero." The corrective is vocabulary discipline, separating **slice** (one piece of functionality cut through the whole application; "more a function than an entity" — *verify a transport order*), **module**, and **bounded context**; calling every area a context sets the separation bar as high as it goes and leaves legitimate connections with no legal answer.

**Why it's worth reading.** Vertical slice architecture is thin in the wiki relative to its weight in the focus, and this is the piece that gives the *positive* rule for cross-slice dependencies — directly load-bearing for whether a slice can serve as an agent's unit of work at all.

### 4. Martin Dilger — "Loop Engineering — Or Why You Should Never Argue With an Agent" (eventmodelers.ai, 2026-06-10)
`raw/articles/dilger-loop-engineering-never-argue-with-agent.md` — *backfilled 08-18, not yet ingested*

**TLDR.** The origin-and-simplicity statement of loop engineering from the Event Modeling camp, tracing the lineage back to Geoffrey Huntley's RALPH loop. The method deflated to four steps: list the tasks, run them one at a time, **record what you learned after each**, then **clear the context completely** so the next iteration starts from scratch with only the learnings. The argument is that long conversations *poison* agents — every correction, reversal and dead end stays in context, and "confusion is what produces hallucination… it's not a random event, it's the predictable" consequence. Hence the title: don't argue, discard and restate.

**Why it's worth reading.** The wiki's `loop-engineering` concept page leans on this piece without a source page behind it, and it is the deliberate minimalist pole against Miracle's three-scale triangle (#1) and swyx's heterogeneous stacking — reading them together is what makes the disagreement legible.

### 5. Rachel Laycock — "The Conductor Developer" (martinfowler.com, 2026-07-31)
`raw/articles/laycock-the-conductor-developer.md` — *backfilled 08-20, not yet ingested*

**TLDR.** Thoughtworks' CTO argues we've been asking the wrong question about AI ("how much faster can it write code?") and watching the wrong bottleneck. She expected the constraint to march down the lifecycle from coding to design to verification, and says plainly: "I was wrong. **AI didn't change what great software looks like. It changed what's scarce. Human attention is now the bottleneck.**" Development starts to feel less like building and more like conducting — the developer's scarce resource is attention allocated across concurrent agents, not keystrokes.

**Why it's worth reading.** It's the executive-altitude version of the same claim Miracle derives mechanically from Amdahl's law and the join gate, and it pairs with her own 08-19 follow-up already in `raw/` — together they'd close the attention-bottleneck thread the KB has been circling from three directions.

### 6. Nick Tune — "Enforced application architecture for agents and humans" (nick-tune.me, 2026-08-13)
`raw/articles/nick-tune-enforced-application-architecture-agents-humans.md` — *filed 08-17, not yet ingested*

**TLDR.** Tune's complaint is that AI-generated code ignores architectural guidance written in skill files, ADRs and markdown, so he replaces guidance with **deterministic enforcement**: classify packages by type (domain-model vs CLI app etc.), prefer broad layer-level import rules (folder A cannot import from folder B), use fine-grained role-based rules only as a last resort, and enforce domain boundaries mechanically. Worked through in an NX TypeScript monorepo with a DSL of package types and roles (`aggregate`, `value-object`, …).

**Why it's worth reading.** This is the "make the boundary a build failure, not a paragraph" answer to Dudycz's ownership rules (#3) — the enforcement layer that decides whether slice boundaries actually survive agent-authored code.
