## Read next

### 1. Oskar Dudycz — "Vertical slices, their ownership and external dependencies"
*event-driven.io, 2026-08-10* · `raw/articles/dudycz-vertical-slices-ownership-and-external-dependencies.md` · **filed, not yet ingested**

**TLDR.** Everything outside a slice — the next folder, the parent module, another module, a third-party API, the database — is one single category: *external*. So a slice should **declare a narrow function type in its own vocabulary** (`CheckContractor = (id) => Promise<'Active'|'Unknown'|'Suspended'>`) instead of importing a wide provider interface, and composition is hand-rolled partial application in one file near the entry point — no container, no registration, no lifetime scopes. Along the way he corrects the vocabulary the KB has been fuzzy on: a **slice** is one piece of functionality cut through the whole app ("more a function than an entity"), a **module** is a logical grouping of slices (criterion: what changes together), and a **bounded context is a linguistic barrier, not a folder** — he says outright he'd rather teams "talk about which functionalities share a vocabulary." Module-level cycles dissolve under this scheme because neither module imports the other, which lets you *defer* an ownership decision instead of making it early and wrong. Persistence splits three ways: business logic per aggregate, read model per query, **schema per module** (a slice is a feature, not a persistence boundary). His closing section turns the locality argument into a context-window argument: a layered structure needing five folders per feature "costs an agent the same way it costs us, faster."

**Why it's worth reading.** It is the best answer the KB now holds to the question `[[vertical-slice-architecture]]` explicitly leaves open — *what does a slice do when it needs something it doesn't own?* — and it does the boundary/ownership work in your top-priority **business capabilities** vocabulary, from a non-.NET, non-vendor voice that independently corroborates `[[miller-codebase-is-the-prompt-vertical-slices-ai]]`.

### 2. Kief Morris — "Humans and Agents in Software Engineering Loops"
*martinfowler.com, "Exploring Gen AI", 2026-03-04* · `raw/articles/morris-humans-and-agents-in-software-engineering-loops.md` · **filed as out-of-window backfill, awaiting your accept/reject**

**TLDR.** Morris splits the **"why loop"** (idea → outcome; humans run it because we're the ones who want what it produces) from the **"how loop"** (the intermediate artefacts — code, tests, tooling, ADRs — which are a means, not deliverables), then notes the how loop is really nested loops: feature → story → code. From there he names three human positions: **outside the loop** (vibe coding, and "some interpretations of Spec Driven Development"), **in the loop** (per-line gatekeeper in the innermost loop → "humans spending more time specifying and reviewing code than they save"), and **on the loop** (build and manage the harness). The sharpest test in the literature: *"The 'in the loop' way is to fix the artefact. The 'on the loop' way is to change the harness that produced the artefact so it produces the results we want."* He also names **"the agentic flywheel"** — a harness that generates recommendations for improving itself, fed by tests/evals, then pipeline stages, production data and commercial results — and closes honestly that at the limit it "might look a lot like humans out of the loop, old-school vibe coding."

**Why it's worth reading.** This is the **missing antecedent** for the KB's whole loop-engineering thread: it predates and independently anticipates `[[addyosmani-own-the-outer-loop]]` (Jul-15) and LangChain's hill-climbing loop by four months. Reading it tells you whether the KB's Thread-4 narrative is a 2026-summer convergence or a rediscovery — which changes how you weight the newer sources.

### 3. `[[agent-readable-model-artifacts]]` — new concept page
`wiki/concepts/agent-readable-model-artifacts.md` · **new wiki page (2026-08-14), tagged `focus`**

**TLDR.** Your own compiled answer to "what form does a design model have to take before an agent can actually use it?" — split out of `event-modeled-agent-design` once the sources accumulated into a pattern. It lays out an **addressability ladder** from freeform whiteboard (nothing reliable) → raw draw.io XML in git → grid coordinates → board-via-MCP → validated file format + linter → format-agnostic export on demand → freeform marks read *and drawn back* → attention markers on screens. Its thesis: what makes a model agent-usable isn't picture-parsing, it's a **stable shared handle** to instruct and report against. It also flags the read-vs-write split (only MCP authoring, drawing-back and `/wdyt` annotations give the agent write access — which is what turns a spec into a conversation surface) and three honest gaps.

**Why it's worth reading.** It's the single densest page in the KB on the EM×agents seam, and it's *yours* — 20 minutes here replaces re-reading a dozen Dilger posts, and its three named gaps are the cleanest research directions currently on the board.

### 4. Rico Fritzsche — "Thinking in Events"
*ricofritzsche.me, 2026-07-03* · `raw/articles/fritzsche-thinking-in-events.md` · **filed 2026-08-07, still not ingested (backlog)**

**TLDR.** The argument is a separation: **Event Modeling is a modeling discipline, Event Sourcing is an implementation choice**, and conflating them "turns a useful way of thinking into another technical prescription." Systems fail, he claims, not from complexity but from *losing the language that explains the complexity* — a conversation about reservations, guests, cancellations and no-shows degrading into records, flags and status columns. Generic verbs are the villain: `ReservationUpdated` "preserves the mutation and loses the reason," hiding whether a guest changed dates, a host cancelled, support corrected an error, or the platform recorded a no-show — situations with different rules, authority, timing and consequences. He grounds the ubiquitous-language claim empirically by pointing at Booking.com's and Airbnb's own public vocabulary, and cites Dymitruk's hotel example for the "imagine the system already exists, ask which facts would be captured" move.

**Why it's worth reading.** It's the cleanest statement of the EM-without-ES position, which matters because the KB's substrate cluster tends to assume the two travel together — and it's been sitting in `raw/` for eight days.

### 5. Oskar Dudycz — "Fixing bugs in Event Sourcing is hard, for real?"
*Architecture Weekly, 2026-07-27* · `raw/articles/dudycz-fixing-bugs-in-event-sourcing.md` · **filed 2026-08-10, still not ingested (backlog)**

**TLDR.** A worked Tuesday-morning scenario: a hotel tourist-tax rate change ships a bug that resolves one rate per reservation from the check-out date, so stays crossing 1 April get charged the new rate for March nights. He walks the **state-based** repair first — the row can't say how its total was produced, `updated_at` reflects a later breakfast add-on rather than the booking, so the migration matches 1,240 rows of which only ~900 are actually broken and there is nothing in the data to separate "this changed" from "this changed because of the bug" — then contrasts what an event log makes recoverable.

**Why it's worth reading.** Concrete, non-evangelical evidence for the *forensic* value of an event log, and a good stress test for the KB's `event-sourcing` page, which is currently long on benefits and short on repair-time war stories.
