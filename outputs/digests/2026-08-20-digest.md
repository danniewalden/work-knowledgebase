## Read next

**1. Martin Dilger — "Loop Engineering - Or Why You Should Never Argue With an Agent"** (eventmodelers.ai, 2026-06-10)
`raw/articles/dilger-loop-engineering-never-argue-with-agent.md` — *filed 08-18, not yet ingested*

**TLDR.** Loop engineering is deliberately simple: a task list, one task per iteration, record the learnings, then **wipe the context completely** and start the next task clean. His mechanism claim is stronger than anything else in the KB: hallucination isn't stochastic, it's *structural* — "confusion is what produces hallucination… the predictable result of a polluted context window" — and the standard reaction (prompt harder, add rules in capitals) makes it worse, because an agent will always agree with your correction while carrying the confusion forward. So: rule broken → no discussion → discard everything except the learnings → retry. The load-bearing line for this wiki is *"Event Modeling Is 'Loop Engineering' Applied"* — the hard part of a loop is defining the task list, and in Event Modeling every slice already **is** a task. He also plants an explicit anti-maximalist flag: the moment you add state, memory and decision-making between iterations, you reintroduce what the loop was designed to remove.

**Why it's worth reading.** This is the single most on-target un-ingested item you have: a tracked primary that states the `loop-engineering` ↔ `vertical-slice-architecture` ↔ `event-modeling` seam directly instead of the KB inferring it — and it's the only recorded **dissent** against the loop-stacking maximalism of `swyx-loopcraft` and the `flue` meta-harness.

**2. Rachel Laycock — "Citizens Build, Agents Execute, Experts Govern"** (martinfowler.com / Rachel's Ramblings, 2026-08-19)
`raw/articles/laycock-citizens-build-agents-execute-experts-govern.md` — *filed today, not yet ingested*

**TLDR.** Thoughtworks' CTO on why "if AI can do this, why aren't our teams 10x faster?" is the wrong question: the gap isn't technology, it's that we taught everyone that *writing software* is the same as *software engineering*. Weekend-built apps are real software; what changes the moment the business depends on them is a different question set (data protection, dependency failure, comprehensibility in two years, audit, 1000×–million× load, knowing something's wrong before customers do). Her claim: the scarce resource is now **engineering judgement**, and experts don't become less important, they become "dramatically more leveraged" — their job shifts to designing "the guardrails, the platforms, the engineering practices and the **feedback loops**." From Thoughtworks' FOSE event: one team spends the day designing a **specification**, agents work overnight, humans review next morning — and "when agents can generate lots of code very quickly, good design matters **more**, not less."

**Why it's worth reading.** Executive-level, non-vendor corroboration of exactly the KB's spec-first / Event-Modeling-as-agent-substrate line, plus a restatement of `harness-engineering` in governance language — useful precisely because it arrives from an audience that doesn't read the EM literature.

**3. Rachel Laycock — "The Conductor Developer"** (same channel, 2026-07-31)
`raw/articles/laycock-the-conductor-developer.md` — *filed today (backfill), not yet ingested*

**TLDR.** The predecessor piece, and the more interesting one. She expected the bottleneck to migrate coding → design → architecture → verification, and says flatly **"I was wrong"** — the bottleneck is **human attention**. The best developers she watches aren't in flow anymore, they're conducting: the conductor's value isn't that the musicians are weak, it's that "someone has to hold the whole system in their head." She reports practitioners running **8 parallel agents — "Ten. Twelve. Beyond that, they become the bottleneck"** — and notes that eight sounded exactly like her own CTO job. The prescription is executive, not technical: protect attention, manage energy not time, reduce unnecessary decisions, build systems that help your brain. Closing question: how do you redesign engineering careers when attention is the scarce resource?

**Why it's worth reading.** An independent field number for `parallel-agent-limit` / `orchestration-tax` from a completely different vantage point (a CTO's org, not a tooling blog), and it reframes the review-bottleneck argument as a *cognitive* constraint rather than a process one.

**4. Simon Willison — "Conceptual integrity and counting lines of code"** (simonwillison.net, 2026-08-19)
`raw/articles/willison-conceptual-integrity-and-counting-lines-of-code.md` — *filed today, not yet ingested*

**TLDR.** Two arguments, lightly edited from his *Talking Postgres* appearance. (a) Lines of code *are* a meaningful agent-productivity signal, because there was a hard human ceiling: 50–60 debugged production lines on a normal day, 200 is "an incredibly good day" — so 1000 same-quality lines/day is a real delta, though reaching it "takes a huge amount of skill and knowledge and experience." The new limiting factor isn't code production but **cognitive capacity**: "I can churn out code a hundred times faster. I don't have the cognitive capacity to stay on top of 100 times the amount of code" — which is why you still need a team, to *load balance* cognitive capacity. (b) What agents erode is Brooks' **conceptual integrity**: cheap features mean "your software grows little weird bumps in funny different directions" → Claire Giordano's Winchester Mystery House (140 rooms, kept building forever). The mechanism, named: discipline used to be enforced by the **cost of time** — "that would take me a week — I cannot justify that." At one hour, the filter is gone.

**Why it's worth reading.** Gives the KB a named, canonical design-integrity concept (`conceptual-integrity`, straight from Brooks) to hang the spec-first argument on, and it converges with Laycock #3 on cognitive capacity from an IC's perspective rather than a CTO's — the three of today's captures form one coherent ingest session on *"what the human is for."*

**5. Nick Tune — "Enforced application architecture for agents and humans"** (nick-tune.me, 2026-08-13)
`raw/articles/nick-tune-enforced-application-architecture-agents-humans.md` — *filed 08-17, not yet ingested*

**TLDR.** The complaint is concrete: agents don't follow architectural guidelines written in skill files, ADRs, or markdown anywhere in the repo — so **enforce the architecture in the build instead**. His iterated approach: classify packages by type (`domain-model` vs CLI app etc.) rather than writing one global ruleset; prefer broad layer-level import rules (folder A cannot import from folder B); use fine-grained role-based rules only as a last resort; and above all **enforce domain isolation** ("if you're only going to add one convention, that's probably the best one"). Shown as a working TypeScript/NX DSL where a `packages/{subdomain}/domain-model` package may contain only code annotated `aggregate | value-object | domain-event | domain-port | domain-service | domain-error`, anything else is a hard build failure, and the only cross-subdomain import allowed is a versioned published language.

**Why it's worth reading.** This is the deterministic-guardrail counterpart to Dilger's static-check gate — it turns "the model is the spec" into something the *compiler* enforces on the agent, which is the missing enforcement layer under the whole slice-ownership / business-capability line.
