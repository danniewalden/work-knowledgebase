### 1. How Event Sourcing Grows With the Business — Rico Fritzsche (ricofritzsche.me, 2026-08-30)
`raw/articles/fritzsche-how-event-sourcing-grows-with-the-business.md` — **filed, not yet ingested**

**TLDR.** Fritzsche inserts a fifth step (*Inspect*) into a running car-rental process — Acquire → Receive → **Inspect** → Infleet → Release — purely to count what has to change. Under a CRUD schema you're altering a shared, global mutable structure and back-filling a status column for every existing row. Under event sourcing you add one event type, one function, and adjust the fold of only those capabilities that actually care; `AcquireVehicle` and `ReceiveVehicle` don't move, and there is no migration because there is nothing to correct. The mechanism he names is the pairing: every action that produces an event *is* a domain capability (`InfleetVehicle` → `VehicleInfleeted`), each capability derives its own state by folding only the events its rules read, so there is no shared vehicle object for two capabilities to fight over. He closes by rejecting the usual justification for event sourcing outright: **history is a consequence of immutability, not the reason for it** — "anyone who views event sourcing as an audit function has misunderstood its purpose."

**Why it's worth reading.** This is the highest-value un-ingested piece for your flagged-important thread: it argues the *code* rung of the four-rung capability table from the inside, and it's a rare source that puts Event Modeling, event sourcing and business capabilities in one causal chain rather than treating them as neighbours. It also directly contradicts the AxonIQ framing in item 6, which is the more interesting reason to have both on the wiki.

### 3. Graph Engineering: Wiring Agents Into an Organization — Andy Wong (awongcm.io, 2026-09-01)
`raw/articles/wong-graph-engineering-wiring-agents-into-an-organization.md` — **filed, not yet ingested**

**TLDR.** The sequel to Wong's loop-engineering post, extending the ladder to context → harness → loop → **graph**: loops govern one agent's campaign, graphs govern which campaign runs next and who may see the result. Every working multi-agent graph decomposes into nodes (agents, deterministic functions, routers, and *human checkpoints as a first-class node type*), edges (a permitted transition, not a suggestion — this is where the org chart is encoded), and shared state that must distinguish **authoritative** artifacts (the actual diff, the actual test output) from convenience summaries, or you get multi-agent telephone. His failure table is the useful part: the reviewer always approves because the review node is secretly the same agent; work vanishes because failure has no defined edge; the human checkpoint gets routed around under load. He is unusually candid that the term began as a partly-satirical Steinberger tweet on 07-18, that LangChain has a fair prior-art claim, and that it was only formalised by an August arXiv survey.

**Why it's worth reading.** It's the clearest statement yet of the layer above loop engineering, and the node/edge/authoritative-state decomposition maps almost one-to-one onto Event Modeling swimlanes and slice boundaries — which is a connection the KB asserts but has thin external support for. Note the maker≠checker failure recurring one level up.

### 4. Vertical Slice Architecture Webinar Recording, and What's Next — Jimmy Bogard (jimmybogard.com, 2026-09-01)
`raw/articles/bogard-vertical-slice-architecture-webinar-recording-whats-next.md` — **filed, not yet ingested**

**TLDR.** A one-minute announcement post that happens to contain the sharpest one-paragraph argument for VSA in the agent era in the whole backlog: we have made *writing* code nearly free and left the cost of *verifying and changing* it exactly where it was. "An agent doesn't read your architecture diagram. It reads your repo and copies what it finds." So structure matters more now, not less — slices hold up under an agent because a change fits in one context window, the blast radius stops at the slice boundary, and the tests still mean something after a refactor. The rest is links to a 700-registrant webinar recording, a six-hour two-part session, and a Zurich workshop on Nov 17–18.

**Why it's worth reading.** Ninety seconds to read, and it supplies the *economic* premise underneath Tune's enforcement mechanism. Also worth reading because yesterday's verify pass caught this exact line being misattributed to Tune on `vertical-slice-architecture` — reading the original is how you keep the two positions separate. The linked webinar recording is uncaptured and is the substantive content.

### 6. Government AI Explainability Requirements: Why Auditable Systems Matter — AxonIQ (2026-08-31)
`raw/articles/axoniq-government-ai-explainability-requirements.md` — **filed, not yet ingested — VENDOR**

**TLDR.** Argues that expanding explainability mandates across ten jurisdictions (EU AI Act, Canada's Directive on Automated Decision-Making, and others) are an *architecture* problem rather than a documentation problem: a CRUD system knows a permit was denied but has overwritten the sequence of conditions and rules that produced the denial, whereas an event-sourced system can replay every event that led to a March 2021 determination in order, with context. "The record is not a byproduct of the system. It is the system."

**Why it's worth reading.** Read it *against* Fritzsche (item 1), who says auditability is a consequence of event sourcing and treating it as the purpose is a misunderstanding. AxonIQ makes auditability the entire selling point. That is a genuine disagreement between two on-thread sources, and the wiki should hold it explicitly. Two markers travel with anything you take from it: **AxonIQ sells an event store**, and its headline "80% audit-prep reduction at a large U.S. bank" is AxonIQ's own unattributed figure with no named customer.
