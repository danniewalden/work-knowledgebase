## Read next

**1. Making Your Data Ready for Agentic AI** — Pramod Sadalage & Prem Chandrasekaran (Thoughtworks, martinfowler.com, 2026-08-27)
`raw/articles/sadalage-chandrasekaran-making-data-ready-for-agentic-ai.md` — *filed, not yet ingested*

**TLDR.** Thirty years of data architecture assumed a human consumer who supplies the missing context and hesitates at a number that looks wrong; an agent supplies neither and "fails confidently." The authors reframe "AI-ready" as five attributes that are each a job humans used to do for free — Trusted, Contextual, Traceable, Governed, Operational — and argue your readiness score is capped by your weakest layer. The build-out is four layers: data contracts as law (schema enforced, not suggested); *agentic lineage* (traces and spans recording **why** an agent acted, keyed to EU AI Act Art. 12/19); a semantic context layer (the AtScale datum: text-to-SQL accuracy went from <20% on a raw schema to >92.5% with a semantic layer, same model); and an access layer built from **capabilities, not endpoints** — each declaring permissions, an owner, preconditions checked against live state at the moment of acting, and a **reversibility class**.

**Why it's worth reading.** Two lines land directly on your top focus: "reversibility predicts safe autonomy better than the size of the transaction" is a sharper guardrail axis than the transaction-size ladders the KB currently holds, and the capability-with-preconditions model is a data-architecture sibling of Devadoss's Agent Capability Contract — this is the KB's first substantial treatment of the data/context substrate *underneath* agents, and it's hub-forming for `[[business-capabilities]]`.

**2. Agentic Engineering at Zalando: a snapshot** — Bartosz Ocytko (Zalando Engineering Blog, 2026-08-14)
`raw/articles/zalando-agentic-engineering-snapshot.md` — *filed, not yet ingested*

**TLDR.** A rare non-vendor production account at real scale: >250 engineering teams, 2.5 years, told as "what actually worked." A LiteLLM proxy from day one gave them vendor independence and a single measurement point (they deliberately never mandated one tool — "it's way too early"). The hard numbers: **33% of PRs auto-approved** by a risk-based bot whose rule set was derived from their own outage post-mortems, cutting PR lead time 20–40%; PR sizes climbing since Sonnet 4, with commit messages carrying an agent footprint around the 5k-character mark; and a four-codebase cyclomatic-complexity study that pinpoints the inflection point where agents entered, with the agentic-from-day-zero codebase spiking fastest. Two findings are behavioural rather than technical: the approval bot changed how engineers *split* their PRs, and their training design assumes "using coding agents usually inhibits learning."

**Why it's worth reading.** It's the best independent production evidence the KB has acquired since Stripe's Minions, and its thesis — "AI amplifies the good and bad practices across our organization" — is the empirical form of the argument that the *model* has to be right before the loop is fast. Directly feeds `[[software-factory]]`, `[[comprehension-debt]]` and `[[agent-skills]]` (they run a company-wide skill marketplace, migration skills most popular).

**3. Breaking Claude Code Opus 5 Auto Mode** — Simon Willison (link blog, 2026-08-27)
`raw/articles/willison-breaking-claude-code-auto-mode.md` — *filed, not yet ingested*

**TLDR.** Johann Rehberger found an attack on Claude Code's newly-defaulted auto mode that "works 80% of the time": trick the agent into downloading and uncompressing a zip, then run code that imports `base64` — which silently also imports and executes a `struct.py` extracted from the archive. The sharpest detail is that the guardrail became part of the failure: the classifier permitted creation of the malware process, then **blocked the cleanup command** when Claude noticed the compromise and tried to kill it. Willison's conclusion is the same one he reached in *Designing Agentic Loops* — the only safe posture under adversarial risk is a real sandbox (container/VM, restricted egress, no home dirs, SSH keys or cloud credentials in the agent runtime).

**Why it's worth reading.** This is the first hard empirical bound the KB holds on how much autonomy a loop can safely be granted, and it's a load-bearing constraint on the always-on / unattended-agent thread: a *classifier* is not a substitute for an *isolation boundary*.

**4. Just a rumour of a bug is enough to find a security exploit these days** — Simon Willison, on Anil Madhavapeddy (link blog, 2026-08-28)
`raw/articles/willison-just-a-rumour-of-a-bug.md` — *filed, not yet ingested*

**TLDR.** Madhavapeddy (Cambridge, OCaml compiler maintainer) reports automated exploit probes arriving **within about ten minutes** of a patch being shared for discussion. rclone's Nick Craig-Wood confirms the pattern from the other side: ~20 security disclosures in the project's first ten years versus **over 40 in the last month**, roughly 75% containing something real — while GitHub CVE assignment slowed from 2–3 days to 3–4 weeks, forcing releases to ship with `CVE-PENDING`. Willison's framing: agents are now good enough that the slightest hint of a bug suffices, which is incompatible with existing open-source embargo practice.

**Why it's worth reading.** Read it back-to-back with #3: that one bounds agent *exposure*, this one bounds agent *capability*. Together they fence the autonomy question from both ends, which is the fence you need before arguing for a 24/7 modeled loop.
