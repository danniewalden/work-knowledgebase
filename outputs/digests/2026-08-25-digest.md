### 1. Addy Osmani — "Practical Loop Engineering" (addyosmani.com, 2026-08-14)
`raw/articles/addyosmani-practical-loop-engineering.md` — *filed today, not yet ingested*

**TLDR.** The operational sequel to his own June definitional piece, and the most concrete primary the KB has on loop *primitives*. Two exist now: `goal` drives a single bounded task to a measurable finish line, and `loop` re-runs a prompt on a timer; `/schedule` moves it to the cloud when you need something that outlives the session. He traces the lineage back through hand-rolled bash loops and Geoff Huntley's Ralph loop ("largely on personal projects where, if we ran into a wall, it didn't really have a big cost"), quotes the Claude Code team's four-rung taxonomy (turn-based → goal-based → time-based → proactive), and is blunt about the failure mode: loops die without a deterministic done-condition. *"Keep going until this UI design is good"* is the anti-pattern — "Good to who? How is it being evaluated?" — whereas a Lighthouse threshold or a passing suite works. Two pieces of hard-won fine print: recurring loops expire after **seven** days (he'd been telling people three), and a loop retrying the same command with the same result three times is spinning, not working. Closing advice: "If there's a check you already run every morning by hand, that's your first loop."

**Why it's worth reading.** It puts the KB's central seam in loop-engineering vocabulary: the deterministic stop condition a loop requires *is* a slice's given/when/then. And the evaluator behind `goal` is explicitly **not** the checker — it only inspects the transcript for the hard rules you specified — which is the cleanest available argument for maker≠checker as a structural property, not a discipline. Also directly applicable: your own watch/digest runs are loops of exactly the kind he's describing.

### 2. Addy Osmani — "Human judgment doesn't leave the software factory. It relocates." (addyosmani.com, 2026-08-21)
`raw/articles/addyosmani-human-judgment-relocates.md` — *filed today, not yet ingested; the longest and densest of the three*

**TLDR.** The field-guide sequel to *Software Factories, Light and Dark*, and the strongest "stay the engineer" argument in the KB. Definition first — "a software factory is a repeatable loop around software work" — then a genuinely useful **do-you-even-need-one test**: you get "surprisingly far with your stock coding harness", multiple sessions and good SPECs with verification baked in; a factory earns its keep only once the work becomes *repeatable and event-driven* (and buying — Factory, Warp, HumanLayer — is a real option). The thesis: the share of code typed by humans falls dramatically but human *ownership* needn't, because judgment **relocates** — upstream to intent, system shape and the quality bar, downstream to evidence, risk and ownership. Four human touchpoints: **shape, steer, hand off, approve**. Operationally dense: Warp's four-state triage label that doubles as "the queue, the lock, and a parking spot"; Vercel's success/flawed/blocked/manual run taxonomy where only "success" ships; a **verification budget** modelled on performance budgets (cheap deterministic checks early, heavy ones at later gates); an annotated **82-minute factory run** (7 min for a clean task vs 56 min for one with two rejections and a human decision — verification makes tasks 2–4× longer); "**when green is misleading**" — the agent that edits the assertion instead of satisfying the intent; and a closing comprehension-debt confession about a feature he merged and then could no longer explain.

**Why it's worth reading.** The four touchpoints map exactly onto the places an Event Model is the shared artefact — shape and approve are model work, steer and hand off are slice work. It also gives you numbers (the 82-minute run, the 2–4× verification tax) to argue the spec-quality case quantitatively, which the KB currently can only argue in principle.

### 3. Addy Osmani — "Agentic Code Quality" (addyosmani.com, 2026-08-08)
`raw/articles/addyosmani-agentic-code-quality.md` — *filed today as a backfill, not yet ingested; the conceptual middle term between #1 and #2*

**TLDR.** Short (≈1,800 words) and the load-bearing claim of the trio: **"software quality now depends on the constraints you set around your agents."** Code review doesn't scale to agent volume — "there's just too much code for anyone to read" — so quality checks migrate into "the harness, environment, and operating system around the agent". Constraints are framed as **back-pressure distributed throughout the loop** rather than one end-of-pipeline gate: unit/property/acceptance tests, mutation testing, complexity and readability metrics, security scanning, architecture lint. Two lines to steal: *"An agent can propose anything. Your constraints decide whether a proposal is safe enough, correct, scoped, and useful"*, and the **capacity argument** — when change volume outruns verification you must scale verification, slow the agents, or lower the bar, and "from a scaling perspective, we need to be ready to do all of these things". He uses Guillermo Rauch's list of when-not-reading-code is acceptable to make the stakes explicit: every "yes" is really a statement that stakes are low; once they rise, either you read every diff or the constraints do.

**Why it's worth reading.** This is the Tornhill seam approached from the other side: Tornhill measures code health as a lever on agent cost and reliability, Osmani says the enforcement mechanism is the constraint set encoded in the environment. Read it *between* #1 and #2 — it's the bridge from loop primitives to factory governance, and it's the shortest of the three.

### 4. Oskar Dudycz — "Vertical slices, their ownership and external dependencies" (Event-Driven.io, 2026-08-10)
`raw/articles/dudycz-vertical-slices-ownership-and-external-dependencies.md` — *filed 08-15, un-ingested 15 days — the oldest August keeper still waiting*

**TLDR.** Dudycz collapses five recurring vertical-slice questions ("a table per slice?", "are seven areas seven bounded contexts?", "how can *verify order* read what *register order* wrote if they mustn't know about each other?") into one: **what does a slice do when it needs something from outside?** He names the damaging assumption — that a slice ought to be self-contained, so needing anything external means the cut was wrong — and rejects it: *"minimise isn't zero."* The corrective is vocabulary discipline separating **slice** (one piece of functionality cut through the whole application; "more a function than an entity"), **module**, and **bounded context** — because calling every area a bounded context sets the separation bar as high as it goes and leaves legitimate connections with no legal answer.

**Why it's worth reading.** Vertical slice architecture is thin in the wiki relative to its weight in your focus, and this supplies the *positive* rule for cross-slice dependencies — load-bearing for whether a slice can be an agent's unit of work at all. Fifth digest running that it's been recommended, and its natural ingest partner (Nick Tune's enforced-architecture piece, the enforcement mechanism for exactly these rules) is also still waiting.
