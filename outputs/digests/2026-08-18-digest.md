### 1. Loop Engineering — Or Why You Should Never Argue With an Agent
**Martin Dilger**, eventmodelers.ai blog, 2026-06-10 · `raw/articles/dilger-loop-engineering-never-argue-with-agent.md` · *filed, not yet ingested*

**TLDR.** Dilger's thesis is that long agent conversations *poison* the context: every correction, reversal and dead end stays in the window, and "confusion is what produces hallucination — not a random event, the predictable result of a polluted context window." So he never argues with an agent. Each iteration gets defined goals and rules; if a rule breaks, he throws away everything **except the recorded learnings** and retries from a cleared context. He then argues Event Modeling *is* loop engineering applied, because the hard part of a loop isn't the loop — it's producing the task list, and EM gives you one for free: every slice is a task with a status (Planned = an agent may claim it), agents subscribe to `slice:changed`, claim, work, and land in Done / Blocked / timed-out. Changing a requirement means moving the slice back to Planned, not prompting. His closing warning is aimed at people like his correspondent who wanted the loop to detect and revert mid-flight task changes: "the moment you start overengineering it — more state, more memory, more decision-making between iterations — you start reintroducing exactly the problems the loop was designed to eliminate."

**Why it's worth reading.** This is the single most on-target source for your focus area in months: a tracked EM voice writing the *authored primary* on loop engineering that the KB has been proxying through Osmani, LangChain and swyx — and it answers the open seam in `[[loop-engineering]]` directly (does an Event Model supply the loop's goals and stop conditions? Dilger: yes, that's the whole point). It also lands a design rule that cuts against the KB's own hill-climbing enthusiasm: *don't* engineer the loop.

### 2. The lights-off software factory is a dead end, if you ask me
**Martin Dilger**, LinkedIn, 2026-08-16 · `raw/notes/dilger-lights-off-software-factory-dead-end.md` · *filed, not yet ingested*

**TLDR.** The Event-Modeling camp's direct answer to Osmani's light-vs-dark software factory. Every team Dilger knows that went lights-off circled back — "it's fun trying to solve an issue, with production burning and no one there to navigate the code base." But full reviews don't scale either: "you just moved the bottleneck." His answer is **layers of trust**: Event Model as spec → GWT scenarios → executable specifications ("call it Test-First Development, as we define the tests long before any code") → then a static-check gate whose four rules are the interesting part — *were any tests adjusted* (review), *are there tests outside the specification* (review), *were files changed outside the Slice Under Development* (**immediate fail**), *were dependencies changed* (review) → then a 2–3 minute **structural** human review, explicitly not functional. Any gate failing means throwing the whole attempt away, recording a "why" learning, and moving the slice back to Planned. He cares about system structure and event shape, not how a slice is implemented internally: "any bad implementation can be easily replaced."

**Why it's worth reading.** It's the KB's first *practitioner* account of escaping the review bottleneck without going dark, and the four static checks are concrete enough to copy. Note the naming collision worth resolving on ingest: he calls the stack **"Triple-Architecture"** here (SDD via EM + slices + event sourcing) while `[[dilger-triplet-flexible-agent-enabled-architecture]]` calls the same thing "the Triplet."

### 3. Enforced application architecture for agents and humans
**Nick Tune**, nick-tune.me, 2026-08-13 · `raw/articles/nick-tune-enforced-application-architecture-agents-humans.md` · *filed, not yet ingested*

**TLDR.** Tune's frustration is that AI-generated code ignores architectural guidelines "written in skill files, ADRs, and various other places in the repo" — so he makes the architecture *executable* instead. His Rivière tooling enforces four layers in an NX TypeScript monorepo: **package classification** (`packages/{subdomain}/domain-model` may contain only `/domain`, and only code annotated `aggregate`/`value-object`/`domain-event`/`domain-port`/`domain-service`/`domain-error` — anything else fails the build); **domain boundaries** (a use-case can import `domain` only from its *own* subdomain, and any package outside the declared layout fails the build); **layer rules** (folder A cannot import folder B; features are vertical slices under `/features/{feature}` with *no* sharing between feature folders); and, as a last resort, **role rules** — the `/adapters` layer may import only code tagged `domain-port` from `/domain`, `/data-access` only `aggregate` and `value-object`, never `domain-service`. His reason is blunt: "AI does dumb stuff like that all the time even with a million lines of markdown screaming at it not to do that." He also constrains role *shape* (value objects: private constructor, branded, `parse*` static, no callable members) so the agent structurally *cannot* hide an aggregate in a value object. Verdict: package/domain/layer rules are cheap and he can't justify skipping them; full role annotation is still an experiment — "come back in 6 months." ADR-002 describes the same architecture for humans and is kept aligned with the executable config.

**Why it's worth reading.** This is the enforcement counterpart to your whole "the model dictates where logic may live" thread — Dilger asserts the model must be enforced because agents drift; Tune shows the build-failing mechanism. It also lands directly on `[[vertical-slice-architecture]]` (features as slices, no cross-feature sharing) and pairs naturally with the un-ingested Dudycz piece on what a slice does when it needs something it doesn't own.

### 4. Trust needs to be engineered ( more than ever with AI )
**Martin Dilger**, LinkedIn, 2026-08-15 · `raw/notes/dilger-trust-needs-to-be-engineered.md` · *filed, not yet ingested*

**TLDR.** Dilger opens with a non-engineer — "probably never wrote a line of code before" — driving Event Modeling boards via AI and MCP and shipping working slices. He grants the objection ("that's just vibe coding, good luck in production") and answers it structurally: the agent never structures the system, it works on a blueprint architecture fixed upfront by the event model and slicing. "All important decisions have been made. The Agent is filling in the blanks." Without the blueprint the agent guesses — usually well, but "it doesn't need to be wrong often to be disastrous": one dependency in the wrong place, a little coupling in another, and you're in a quality spiral you can only catch by reviewing everything. "AI can write good code. The thing is, you just can't trust it to do so reliably. The real issue is trust." Modeling upfront isn't bureaucracy, it's *engineering trust* — working around the review bottleneck by making most reviews obsolete rather than doing more of them.

**Why it's worth reading.** Read it right after #2 (they're two halves of one argument) — and read it as the sharpest available answer to the objection the KB currently has no evidence against: Alvis Ng's "a spec authored solo is a contract nobody else signed." Dilger's non-developer shipping slices is either the counter-example or, on a less generous reading, exactly Ng's flattening with the author being the *only* voice in the room. Worth your judgment on ingest.

### 5. React for Agents: Astro Creator Brings Hooks to his Meta-Harness, Flue
**Richard MacManus interviewing Fred Schott**, Latent.Space, 2026-08-15 · `raw/articles/macmanus-schott-react-for-agents-flue-meta-harness.md` · *filed, not yet ingested*

**TLDR.** Schott (Astro's creator, now at Cloudflare) shipped Flue 2 around React-style **Agent Hooks**: an agent is a JS function that "re-renders on every turn," and 16 built-in hooks (`useSkill()`, `useTool()`, `useSubagent()`) let it change its own configuration mid-conversation — needed for "real support bots, real triage bots" that can't be configured in advance. The framing quote for the KB: **"Our early bet was that the harness is actually not a feature, but it's fundamental to what you think an agent is. There is no agent without a harness."** He also reports that file-based routing was an antipattern (customers have *one* agent, not five routes), that Flue is an opinionated layer over **Pi**, a minimal open-source harness (Pi:Flue as Vite:Astro), that Vercel's **eve** is the directly comparable harness-first framework while the "OG frameworks" (AI SDK, Agents SDK, Mastra) are retrofitting harnesses as features, and that the **meta-harness** term (Databricks Omnigent, the self-improving Exo) is still ill-defined.

**Why it's worth reading.** Thread 4 has been arguing "Agent = Model + Harness" from practitioner essays; this is a framework author betting a product on the stronger claim that the harness *constitutes* the agent, plus the first named entities for the harness-as-substrate layer (Pi, eve, Omnigent, Exo) that the KB has no pages for.

### 6. Two arXiv papers on harness self-improvement (Evo-Bench + SBCO)
**Huang et al., arXiv:2608.09096** and **Kulkarni et al., arXiv:2608.10157**, both 2026-08-10 · `raw/papers/evo-bench-can-language-models-improve-agent-harness.md`, `raw/papers/sbco-verifier-grounded-harness-optimization.md` · *filed, not yet ingested — abstracts only, full PDFs not retrieved*

**TLDR.** **Evo-Bench** is the first benchmark for *harness evolution* — a model's ability to autonomously optimize its own operating harness — deliberately constructed to isolate harness gains from base-model strength (auxiliary-task evolution to find harness-sensitive tasks, then sensitivity-aware stratified splitting). Across nine models, top gains reach **16.6 points**, approaching hand-engineered baselines; autonomous evolution beats human harnesses on General and Search tasks but **struggles on Office tasks** needing highly specific workflows, shows "early saturation" anomalies, and produces harnesses that transfer as reusable reasoning structures across policy models. **SBCO** attacks the cost side: instead of the Darwin/Huxley Gödel-machine style of *self-referential* self-improvement (which only works where task competence and self-modification competence coincide — i.e. coding), it learns a decomposed **bank of verifiers** plus a harness policy by block-coordinate ascent, self-supervised with no human labels and a fixed meta-agent — matching or beating a self-modifying baseline at **4–5.5× less compute**.

**Why it's worth reading.** These are the second and third pieces of *independent* evidence for the hill-climbing loop after AHE, and they're the antidote to the "vendor/practitioner framing dominates" caveat sitting on the loop-engineering strand. SBCO in particular is the grader/verifier loop given a formal treatment and the cheapest known instance of maker≠checker. Both are abstract-only in `raw/` — enough to cite, not enough to lean on.
